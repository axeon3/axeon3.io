Team FAQ

Frequently asked questions about Team Axe, our Dynamic Coordination workflow, and our Lunar Landing Site Navigator & Hardware Protection Engine.

Team Axe is an interdisciplinary aerospace software and telemetry engineering team formed for the NASA Space Apps Challenge (San Jose). We developed the Lunar Landing Site Navigator & Hardware Protection Engine—a decision-support and telemetry validation system designed to identify safe lunar touchdown zones while protecting spacecraft hardware from hazardous slopes, boulders, thermal stress, and corrupted sensor data.
Instead of assigning static titles or hierarchical "Project Lead" roles, Team Axe operates with a flat, high-trust Dynamic Coordination workflow. Every team member wears different hats—contributing across telemetry ingestion, topographic analysis, safety engine rules, UI engineering, and validation testing. Responsibilities adapt dynamically based on real-time engineering priorities.
In mission-critical telemetry processing, dID represents the four primary categories of telemetry anomalies our assurance engine screens for before guidance commands are executed:
  • Discrepancies: Conflicting values between redundant sensors (e.g., star tracker vs. IMU).
  • Inconsistencies: Temporal drift or physical impossibility across sequential telemetry frames.
  • Incomplete Data: Dropped downlink packets, truncated elevation tiles, or missing metadata headers.
  • Incorrect Data: Out-of-bounds readings, calibration bias errors, or corrupted bitfields.
The Hardware Protection Engine cross-references local lunar topography (slope angles, crater depth-to-diameter ratios, and boulder fields) against the physical mechanical tolerances of the lander—such as landing gear tilt limits, engine bell clearance, and thermal dissipation thresholds. If a target zone exceeds safe limits or exhibits unverified dID telemetry faults, the engine flags an immediate divert recommendation to a pre-validated fallback zone.
